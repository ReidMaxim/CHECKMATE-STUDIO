# Mission 005.1 · The Story First

## Why the F5 film fell flat

The producer's `evaluate` promotes decision-contrast stories at 70 and upward. A small board and the same knight taking two routes added clarity and visual points to the F5 candidate, even though the recorded bishop capture does not by itself tell the viewer why the choice mattered. The resulting 73-point score measured teachability and ease of depiction without proving a satisfying consequence. The story stopped at a comparison.

The offline `develop` replaces the first line with a generic choice prompt. Broadcast replaces that line again with “Where would you put this knight?” and adds a new beat for the bishop capture. That creates a clear move replay, but the viewer has little reason to commit to a square before they know what either side is trying to achieve. The final comparison reopens the initial question instead of resolving it. The 42-second cut spends time establishing and resetting two boards, and spoken square names do work the graphics already do.

Hosted writing still uses a fixed shot list, one to four sentences per shot, and complete fact coverage in each spoken slot. It can write better prose, but enters after the central premise and the spoken beats have been decided. It cannot remove an unnecessary beat. The hosted client requires OPENROUTER_API_KEY and STORY_MODEL and caps requested prompt and completion pricing at zero. The prior cloud run reported offline composition. The new workflow records configuration status without exposing secret values and stores any successful free hosted draft separately for editorial review.

## Pitch review before rendering

Editorial verdicts depend on a focused question, a causal payoff the viewer can see, and a route to explaining it within 60 seconds. Engine swing plays no part in this review.

| **Why Give Up Both Bishops?** · Lasker–Bauer, Amsterdam 1889 | White gives up two bishops to remove the pawns beside Black's king. The second capture is surprising because it does not give check. The tension is whether White can exploit the exposure after both bishops are gone. | Establish two shielding pawns. Show the queen chase and the rook taking two steps to deliver a check that Black answers by blocking with the queen. White takes it and Black recaptures the rook. | **One Move, Two Checks** · Réti–Tartakower, Vienna 1910 | White gives up the queen and the rook move produces double check. Either legal king reply allows a bishop mate. | Show the queen capture and draw out the two separate attacking lines. The bishop's mate provides a clean visual answer. Approximately 30–40 seconds. | **Hold.** Strong puzzle, but another capture that opens a route followed immediately by mate is close to the Opera benchmark. Both replies would add a rewind. The played reply and mate suffice for a short proof. |

End with the consequences of the hunt rather than implying that the game became forced after the first capture. ## Script review

[Two complete scripts and their beat references](../examples/story-first/lasker-bauer.json). Version A asks about material and lets the rook supply the answer. Version B points to the rook first and invites viewers to figure out how it will get into the attack. A wins this editorial pass because it keeps the rook reveal in reserve and gives the second bishop its own beat. The two voice scripts are editorial work prepared offline. They are not generated by a new game-specific writing template.

Each spoken thought cites recorded SAN or an asserted position. Replay verifies both. Interpretation is deliberately limited to what the camera shows. In particular, the narration does not claim the king captures were forced, does not call the finish checkmate, and does not attribute an intention to either player. The optional pinned comment asks who joins the attack after both bishops are gone. No spoken closing CTA.

## Benchmark comparison

*What the Capture Opens* gives the viewer a specific visual problem and one decisive change in the geometry. The Story First cut borrows that pacing principle and gives viewers a new problem. Here two separate captures remove the king's cover, and the unexpected payoff belongs to a rook that began on the back rank. This is a longer, more move-dense sequence and needs a viewing check around the queen chase. Against *The Knight Choice on F5*, it stays on the recorded game and answers its opening question with a material consequence. The producer keeps the established branded opening and chessboard renderer. George's delivery and the clarity of the faster silent moves need human review.
